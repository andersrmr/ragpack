# TinyAgent conversation

## You

What is the core functionality that distinguishes between a mere LLM and an agent?

## TinyAgent

A **mere LLM** generates a response to the text it receives. An **agent** uses an LLM (or another decision-making system) to choose what to do next in pursuit of a goal, then responds to what happens.

In a simple chat setting, the “environment” is the conversation: TinyAgent can use its conversation memory to interpret the latest message and decide on a helpful reply. The key difference is the **goal-directed decision loop**, not simply having an LLM inside. An agent may take actions beyond replying, but tools aren’t required to understand the basic idea.

## You

But can you have an agent without tools?

## TinyAgent

Yes. **Tools are optional.** A chat agent can act only by choosing what to say next—for example, answering, asking a clarifying question, or using conversation memory to tailor its reply.

In TinyAgent, the LLM produces the text, while memory provides context and the chat interface carries the exchange. What makes it agent-like is that it uses that context to choose a response toward a goal—not whether it can call external tools. Definitions of “agent” vary, but tool use isn’t required in the basic sense.

## You

Give me a clear example of TinyAgent behaving like an agent without tools.

## TinyAgent

Suppose you ask TinyAgent:

> **You:** Help me practice fractions. Give me one problem at a time, and don’t reveal the answer before I try.

TinyAgent replies:

> **TinyAgent:** What is \(1/2 + 1/4\)? Give it a try.

You answer:

> **You:** \(2/6\).

TinyAgent uses the conversation history to notice the likely mistake—adding the denominators—and responds:

> **TinyAgent:** Not quite. When adding fractions, keep the denominator the same if the fractions can be rewritten with a common denominator. Can you rewrite \(1/2\) in fourths?

Here’s how its basic parts contribute:

- **Conversation memory** keeps the practice goal and the previous answers in context.
- **The LLM wrapper** uses that context to choose a helpful next reply.
- **The chat interface** carries the messages back and forth.

TinyAgent uses no tools here: its actions are just the choices it makes in the conversation, such as giving a problem, offering a hint, or asking the next question.

## You

And that behavior is driven by the nature of the system prompt + user prompt, correct?  That plus the "harness" or LLM wrapper enable it to act in an agentic way, right?

## TinyAgent

Yes—with one important nuance: the **prompts guide the behavior**, and the **wrapper/harness makes that behavior happen across turns**.

- The **system prompt** can set a role or goal, such as “tutor the learner one problem at a time.”
- The **user prompt** gives the immediate request, such as “Help me practice fractions.”
- **Conversation memory** lets TinyAgent use earlier messages, including the learner’s answers.
- The **LLM wrapper** sends the relevant instructions and history to the model, then returns its response through the chat interface.

Together, these can produce agent-like behavior without tools. But a prompt alone doesn’t make a system an agent: it needs to use the ongoing context to choose what to do next. In TinyAgent, that choice may simply be whether to give a problem, a hint, or a follow-up question.

## You

ok, so TinyAgent orchestrates this loop.  It is essential to the agent-like loop behavior.

## TinyAgent

Yes. **TinyAgent orchestrates the loop**: it gathers the current message and conversation history, sends them to the LLM, and returns the LLM’s reply to the chat. That loop lets the agent-like behavior continue across turns.

The parts contribute differently: the **LLM chooses a response**, while TinyAgent’s **wrapper and memory provide the process and context** for making that choice. The wrapper is essential to *this implementation* of the ongoing chat loop, though other systems can organize the loop differently.
